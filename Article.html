The Age of Artificial Intelligence Has Only Just Begun

From language models that write poetry to vision systems that detect cancer earlier than any doctor, AI has moved from science fiction to daily infrastructure faster than almost anyone predicted. But the real revolution, experts warn, is still ahead of us.

By Kasun Perera | June 2026 | 8 min read

From Curiosity to Infrastructure

Not long ago, "artificial intelligence" conjured images of robots and science fiction villains. Today it powers the search bar you typed into this morning, the spam filter that protected your inbox, and the traffic navigation app that rerouted you around that accident on the highway. AI has quietly become as foundational as electricity.

The shift accelerated sharply between 2022 and 2025, when large language models became publicly available and showed that machines could hold conversations, write software code, summarise legal documents, and even compose music — tasks that once seemed uniquely human. The question is no longer whether AI will transform industry. The question is: which industries will transform fastest?

"We are not building tools anymore. We are building collaborators — entities that think alongside us."

How Large Language Models Actually Work

At their core, large language models (LLMs) are statistical engines trained on enormous libraries of text. Given a prompt, they predict what word is most likely to come next, and then the word after that, and so on. What makes modern LLMs remarkable is scale: billions of learnable parameters tuned across trillions of words allow them to develop something that looks very much like understanding.

The training process involves two main stages. In pre-training, the model reads vast datasets — books, websites, scientific papers, code repositories — and adjusts its internal weights to become better at predicting text. In fine-tuning, human trainers guide the model toward helpful, accurate, and safe responses.

AI in Healthcare: A Quiet Revolution

In hospitals across the world, AI diagnostic tools are reading radiology scans and flagging anomalies faster than any human radiologist could. At the University of Oxford, a deep learning model trained on chest X-rays consistently detects early-stage lung cancer with accuracy that matches or exceeds senior specialists.

Drug discovery is another frontier. Traditional drug development takes ten to fifteen years and costs billions of dollars. AlphaFold, developed by Google DeepMind, solved the protein-folding problem — predicting the 3D structure of proteins from their amino acid sequence — in a feat many considered decades away.

The Risks We Cannot Ignore

For all the promise, the risks are serious. AI systems can inherit and amplify the biases present in their training data. A hiring algorithm trained on historical data might disadvantage women or minority candidates if past hiring was biased.

Misinformation is another growing threat. Generative AI makes it trivial to produce convincing fake images, audio, and video — so-called deepfakes. As the quality of synthetic media improves, distinguishing real from fabricated content becomes harder for ordinary citizens and even for trained journalists.

The European Union's AI Act, passed in 2024, created the world's first comprehensive legal framework for AI risk, banning certain high-risk applications outright while requiring transparency and human oversight for others.

What Comes Next

The next frontier is agentic AI — systems that don't just answer questions but take actions in the world: booking your flights, managing your calendar, writing and running software on your behalf. Early versions exist today; researchers expect significantly more capable versions within two to three years.

For now, the most honest assessment is this: AI has already changed the world in ways we are still measuring, and the change is accelerating. The age of AI has not peaked. It has barely started.
